\section{Introduction}

Automatic analysis of literary narratives is now an important research area in Natural Language Processing (NLP). NLP encompasses computational methods that enable machines to analyze, understand, and manipulate human language. When applied specifically to the linguistic study of texts, it is often referred to as Natural Language Processing for texts (TALN). Among its applications, automatic generation of character networks is particularly compelling. The goal is to identify the characters in a narrative, recognize the different forms by which they are mentioned, and detect the relations they maintain in order to build a graph that represents their social network. Such an approach makes it possible to study narrative structure automatically, visualize interactions between protagonists, and extract relational dynamics that are difficult to observe at scale through manual reading. However, this task raises several important challenges. One of the main difficulties lies in alias and coreference resolution. A single character can be referred to in many different ways throughout a narrative: full name, first name, title, nickname, or pronoun. For example, the mentions “Hari Seldon,” “Seldon,” or “the psychohistorian” may all refer to the same individual.

To evaluate the performance of our system, we used the Kaggle platform, which provides an automatic evaluation environment. The studied corpus is associated with a scoring system that compares the graphs generated by our program with a reference solution. The score accounts for false positives and false negatives to produce an overall percentage of correct predictions.

This article presents the main stages in the design of our automatic character network generation system. First, we provide a state-of-the-art review of existing methods for narrative network extraction and NLP. We then detail the adopted methodology, including corpus preprocessing, the use of NER tools, alias resolution via graph modeling, and relation identification. We present the results obtained on the studied corpora and their evaluation via Kaggle, before discussing the limits of our approach and possible improvement directions. You will find the public GitHub repository for our project here: \url{https://github.com/evan-massol/characters-network-extraction-AMS}.

\section{State of the Art}

Since the first formal attempts, character network extraction has largely relied on a pipeline approach structured into successive stages: text segmentation into relevant units, detection and normalization of character entities, alias and coreference resolution, and finally construction and weighting of the resulting graph. The foundational work by Elson, Dames, and McKeown (2010) illustrates this supporting approach, notably through simple heuristics such as character co-occurrence in the same scene or dialog identification to infer direct interactions. These heuristics are robust and relatively easy to implement, especially on well-structured corpora. However, they assume that textual proximity reflects a true social relation, which often leads to approximations or artificial relations, particularly in complex literary texts where narration can be indirect or fragmented.

A major challenge in building character networks is reliable recognition of fictional entities. This task primarily relies on Named Entity Recognition (NER). Classical approaches based on statistical models or recurrent LSTM neural networks have shown good performance on journalistic texts, but their effectiveness decreases significantly in the literary setting. This limitation is explained by stylistic diversity, frequent use of nicknames, paraphrases, or indirect references to characters. Recent advances rely on pretrained Transformer language models, such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), which provide stronger contextualization and improve character detection in complex narratives.

Nevertheless, recognizing entities alone is insufficient without effective coreference resolution. Coreference aims to group different linguistic mentions (proper names, pronouns, nominal descriptions) that refer to the same character. The work by Lee et al. (2017) proposed end-to-end neural models for coreference resolution, now widely adopted and adapted to literary corpora, because they directly condition the structural coherence of the extracted network.

Once characters are identified and normalized, relation extraction becomes another central problem. The simplest methods rely on co-occurrence within the same textual unit, a widely used but limited hypothesis because it does not distinguish the nature, intensity, or direction of relations. More advanced approaches attempt to exploit dialog, communication verbs, or syntactic structures to extract more semantic relations, such as familial, hierarchical, or conflictual links.

Recent trends show a shift toward hybrid pipelines that combine robust heuristics with neural modules, along with integration of specialized pretrained models (BERT, SpanBERT) for NER, coreference, and relation extraction. Sims and Bamman (2020) in particular propose graph-based methods to model information propagation and the temporal dynamics of character interactions. Moreover, the emergence of Graph Neural Networks opens new perspectives to refine the final relational structure and capture network evolution throughout the narrative. In summary, automatic character network extraction is a complex task that requires integrating multiple NLP subtasks. Despite significant advances, especially through pretrained language models, many challenges remain, fully justifying ongoing research and the development of systems better adapted to literary texts.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Text Preprocessing}

In his works, Isaac Asimov uses specific linguistic devices, such as intentionally truncated words, to immerse the reader in the atmosphere of ancient manuscripts or incomplete narratives. While these stylistic choices enrich the narrative dimension, they pose significant obstacles for automatic text analysis. Additional difficulties include elements external to the narrative—such as page numbers—characters misinterpreted during extraction, and the systematic absence of every other page due to copyright constraints.

In this context, it is essential to rework the initial textual material to obtain a more homogeneous corpus, closer to standard language and thus easier to process with NLP tools. To meet this challenge, a preprocessing step was integrated into our pipeline. It aims to remove or transform the identified problematic elements, with the goal of ensuring reliable downstream processing.

The available .pdf files must first be converted to .txt files. This operation nevertheless preserves various noisy elements such as page numbers, section titles, and chapter headings. These can be removed effectively with regular expressions (regex).

A particularly important aspect of preprocessing concerns the structure of the provided corpora, which contain only one page out of two. This constraint fragments some sentences, leaving some without their beginning and others without their end. These incomplete segments, which may lack a subject or verb, are difficult to analyze automatically and can degrade output quality. Therefore, a methodological choice is required: should these truncated sentences be preserved to keep as much data as possible, or removed to exclude lower-quality text? Given our still limited mastery of NLP tools, we chose, at this stage of the project, to remove these fragments.

We also observed better results on the Kaggle corpus when removing line breaks and replacing the characters “ ’ ” with the standard apostrophe “ ' ”. After applying this change to all files in that corpus, our Kaggle score increased from 0.41 to 0.50. The origin of this phenomenon likely lies in the training data used by the NLP pipeline we employ, which we discuss later. If its training texts contain only standard apostrophes “ ' ”, the character “ ’ ” is unknown and degrades results. We found no information on the training data for this NLP pipeline, so this remains a hypothesis.

\subsection{NER Analysis}

NLP tools are based on complex architectures, most of them relying on deep neural networks that require large volumes of training data. Designing and training such models is a substantial effort that could, by itself, justify a full research project. However, given the objectives of this work, it was more relevant to reuse existing libraries than to develop dedicated models. In this perspective, we used a well-established library known for its performance, providing a complete processing chain including, for our purposes: a tokenizer—program for extracting textual units such as words or punctuation—a POS tagger—program for identifying the nature of a token—and a NER model trained to recognize entities such as people, places, or currencies based on the context of words.

In our first trials, it seemed relevant to compare different existing tools. In this context, we compared two well-known libraries that provide complete processing pipelines. To evaluate the effectiveness of spaCy (Explosion AI, 2024) and Stanza (Qi et al., 2020), we created a short narrative of about ten lines, including direct and indirect dialogs. This text was then processed independently by each library. The results are shown in part in Figure 1 and analyzed below.

The analysis highlights overall more precise tokenization with spaCy. By contrast, Stanza tends to group multiple textual elements into a single token, which leads to notable errors. These inaccuracies seem primarily related to imperfect punctuation handling. Differences also appear in character recognition: for example, spaCy classifies the expression “ministre de la Santé” as an organization rather than a character. While this error is debatable, it remains marginal compared to the tokenization issues observed with Stanza. Thus, spaCy proved better suited to the needs of this project and was retained for subsequent work.

On small corpora, the spaCy library performs particularly well in constructing a list of token groups corresponding to different character mentions. The library produces few clearly inconsistent errors, such as identifying a verb as a character. Nevertheless, when processing longer texts—especially within Asimov corpora—some anomalies appear. To limit these errors, we use an anti-dictionary: a list of terms explicitly excluded from recognition as characters. This dictionary was progressively enriched during program development as new errors were identified. However, its use must remain measured and targeted, since there is no guarantee that an author follows standard naming conventions for characters. Isaac Asimov provides an emblematic example by naming one of his characters “Dors,” a term that, out of context, could be interpreted as a common noun in English or a verb in French. For example, we noticed that the NER regularly identified imperatively conjugated verbs as character aliases, so we added these problematic verbs to our anti-dictionary.

\subsection{Alias Resolution}

After spaCy’s NER processing, we obtain a large list of aliases. The goal of this pipeline stage is to identify which character each alias belongs to. Initially, we developed a program based on simple rules derived from first and last names. The idea was to select the longest tokens of each alias and identify matches with other aliases. Two aliases sharing the same token were therefore linked to the same character.

In this new perspective, each named entity detected by the NER module becomes a node in a graph. The objective is no longer only to associate lexically similar forms, but to systematically evaluate the probability that two distinct entities actually refer to the same character. For each pair of named entities, two types of similarity are computed in a weighted sum to produce a final score.

The first is orthographic similarity, based on standard textual distance measures. This step identifies relatively obvious cases such as “Hari Seldon” and “Seldon,” as well as minor variations. We use the RapidFuzz library (2026), which itself uses the Indel distance. The second similarity considered is contextual similarity. It compares the linguistic contexts in which entities appear. Concretely, for each occurrence of an entity, the surrounding context is vectorized with the Sentence-transformers library (2026). Two entities with similar contexts—for example, performing the same actions, in the same places, or alongside the same characters—have a higher probability of referring to the same individual.

If two aliases reach a sufficient similarity score, their nodes are linked. Once the graph is built, the next step is to identify clusters, i.e., strongly connected subsets. For this, we use the Networkx library (2025) and its Louvain community algorithm, which maximizes intra-group connections while minimizing inter-group connections. Each identified group then represents a unique character, aggregating all of its aliases. This approach handles complex cases more robustly, especially when lexical variants are indirectly connected.

Because the proposed solution is complex, it is necessary to set several parameters. These include: minimum thresholds for the textual score, contextual score, and final score, as well as the weights of the textual and contextual scores when computing the final score. It is also possible to choose the clustering resolution—a parameter specific to the Networkx (2025) algorithm. These parameters allow a form of customization and multiple possible outcomes. Using the score provided by Kaggle after each submission, we were able to evaluate which configurations were among the best. Figure 2 compiles different configuration trials we tested.

\subsection{Relation Identification}

Relation identification is a difficult task in character network generation. After correctly identifying characters, it is necessary to assess whether two individuals are interacting in the narrative, regardless of the positions of their aliases in the text. For simplicity, the guidelines given to us define relations in positional terms only. That is, two characters are considered related if one of their aliases appears within 25 tokens of an alias of the other.

After developing a solution to identify relations between characters, we sought to refine this processing by identifying the nature of the relation. The idea is to assign a value to the relation context by vectorizing, with Sentence-transformers (2026), the meaning of the richest words in the sentence. Thanks to the POS tagger, we could separate nouns, verbs, and adjectives from other tokens. For each relation we computed an average vector of the meaningful words in the context.

In parallel, we established a typology of relations: family, romantic, professional. For each category, we listed several dozen verbs, nouns, and adjectives with a semantic relation, then compressed them into an average reference vector. For each relation found in the text, we compared its average vector to all reference vectors using a dot product. Our objective was to identify the closest match and thus assign the relation to the appropriate category.

\section{Results}

Our best Kaggle submission achieved a score of 57\%. Based on the Kaggle score, our results are interesting, but their quality is still too low to claim an adequate solution to the challenges of character graph generation.

\section{Discussion}

As discussed in the previous section, our results are open to criticism on several aspects. First, the average Kaggle score we obtained is explained by both false positives and false negatives in our results. The NER, whose task is to identify aliases, is therefore limited. Even in pre-tests we observed some errors. However, given the complexity of such a tool, it is difficult to customize it for our corpora. Thus, we use the anti-dictionary, a minimal safeguard to filter false positives. However, this tool is also open to criticism, notably because of the significant effort required to configure it, which makes it look more like a manual tool than an automatic one. The required effort does not guarantee that the solution is suitable for all corpora. If a character appears in one book, their aliases may be used to name other elements in another work. Thus, the anti-dictionary acts more as a fallback tool to mitigate the limits of our NER. If we removed its use from our solution, we could lose around ten percentage points on the Kaggle score.

The errors explaining our low Kaggle score also lie in alias resolution. If aliases belonging to the same character are not linked, this produces a false negative, and vice versa. Given the complexity of our solution, the best way to improve our score would be to test all possible configurations, which could not be done due to time constraints. Parameters concerning the minimum textual and contextual scores, as well as the resolution of the Louvain algorithm, were only briefly tested. Similarly, it would be useful to compare results using different clustering algorithms.

Finally, regarding relation categorization, we saw earlier that one category seemed to dominate. After vector analysis of the constructed reference vectors, we observed that they were at small distances from each other. This proximity did not allow a clean partitioning of the vector space. We interpret this phenomenon with two causes. The first is that we did not collect enough category-specific words. In other words, the collected words were too general and could be used in all the defined relational contexts. For example, the term “love” could be used in a romantic relation as well as a family relation. This genericity causes category confusion. We also note that the vocabularies associated with romance and family themes are relatively close. By contrast, the professional category is more distinct and therefore less affected by confusion, which explains its over-representation. The second cause could be the too limited number of collected words specific to each category. It would be useful to increase their quantity to ensure representative reference vectors. In addition to the potential improvements mentioned, it would be more rigorous to label a relation as neutral when its vector representation is too far from all reference vectors. This solution would require additional testing phases to set a threshold.

\section{Conclusion}

The results show that automatic construction of character graphs is a particularly complex task. Despite the use of high-performing NER tools, contextual vectorization methods, and clustering algorithms, many errors remain, especially in alias resolution and relation categorization. The maximum score of 57\% obtained on Kaggle illustrates both the interest of our approach and its current limits. The difficulties encountered arise largely from the specific nature of literary corpora: stylistic diversity, implicit references, nicknames, lexical ambiguities, and the incomplete structure of the provided texts.

Nevertheless, this work also highlights the value of hybrid approaches that combine heuristics, vector representations, and graph modeling. The joint use of orthographic and contextual similarities for alias resolution proved relevant for handling some complex cases that simple rules struggle with. Likewise, the produced visualizations provide a usable representation of relational dynamics in the studied narratives. Several improvement directions clearly emerge. It would be particularly relevant to explore other NER models specialized for literary corpora, or to further refine coreference resolution strategies. Enriching the reference vectors used for relation categorization could also improve semantic separation between classes.

Beyond the results obtained, this project helped us better understand the technical and methodological challenges related to automatic narrative analysis. It shows that character network generation remains an active research field, at the intersection of computer science, linguistics, and literary analysis, where many challenges remain to be addressed.

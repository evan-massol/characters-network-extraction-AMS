\begin{abstract}
This report presents our current progress on a project focused on automatic extraction of character networks from Isaac Asimov's literary works.
Natural Language Processing techniques, especially Named Entity Recognition and relation analysis, are used to identify characters and their interactions. 
The project currently addresses key challenges such as alias disambiguation and automatic identification of relation types.
\end{abstract}

\section{Context}

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field of computer science and computational linguistics that aims to enable machines to understand, analyze, and generate human language. Among its many applications, extracting character networks from narrative texts is a specific challenge that combines Named Entity Recognition (NER) and relation analysis. These techniques make it possible to represent interactions between characters as graphs, which is useful for literary analysis, plot study, and visualization of narrative dynamics.

In this project, we focused on several corpora taken from Isaac Asimov's works. The proposed challenge is to automatically extract characters, handle alias-related issues, identify relationships between characters, and characterize the nature of those relationships. This broad framework reveals many complex aspects, especially those related to the narrative and stylistic nature of the studied texts.

Several major difficulties were identified through our analyses. The first concerns named entity recognition. NLP tools must correctly distinguish characters from other entity types such as organizations, locations, or concepts, which can be difficult because of ambiguous wording, honorific titles, or compound names. The second difficulty lies in alias management, meaning the different ways the same character can be referred to in the narrative. Variation in first names, last names, nicknames, and cases where multiple characters share similar names creates an additional obstacle to automatic extraction.

This report presents our methodology and current progress in this project. It details the technical choices made for text preprocessing, implementation of character recognition algorithms, alias resolution, and relation detection and classification. It also highlights current limitations and improvement directions identified for future phases.

For more details and access to the code developed in this project, a GitHub repository is available at the following address: https://github.com/evan-massol/characters-network-extraction-AMS. If you need an authorization to access the repository, please contact us by email or during class.

\section{Current State}

This section presents the main stages of the current pipeline and summarizes the current progress for each stage.

\subsection{Text Preprocessing}

In his works, Isaac Asimov uses specific linguistic devices, such as intentionally truncated words, to immerse readers in the atmosphere of ancient manuscripts or incomplete narratives. While these stylistic choices enrich narrative depth, they also create significant obstacles for automatic text analysis. Additional difficulties include elements external to the narrative, such as page numbers, incorrectly interpreted characters during extraction, and the systematic absence of every other page because of copyright constraints.

To address these issues, we integrated a preprocessing stage in our pipeline. Its objective is to remove or transform problematic elements to ensure reliable downstream processing.

The available PDF files must first be converted into TXT files. This operation still preserves noisy elements such as page numbers, section titles, and chapter headings. These are removed using regular expressions.

A major issue is that provided corpora contain only one page out of two. This fragments many sentences, leaving some without beginnings and others without endings. These incomplete segments, often lacking a subject or a verb, are difficult to analyze automatically and can degrade output quality. At this stage, we chose to remove these fragments rather than preserve them.

We also observed better results on the Kaggle corpus when removing line breaks and replacing non-standard apostrophe variants with the standard apostrophe. After applying this modification to all files in that corpus, our Kaggle score increased from 0.41 to 0.50.

So far, only one corpus has undergone full preprocessing: the Sample Foundation corpus. Each corpus appears to follow its own logic, both in page organization and in regular expressions needed to identify page numbers, chapters, or sections. Preprocessing scripts must therefore be adapted corpus by corpus.

\subsection{NER}

NLP tools rely on complex architectures, most of them based on deep neural networks requiring large amounts of training data. Designing and training such models is substantial work. Given the objectives of this project, we chose to reuse existing libraries rather than train custom models.

In this perspective, we used spaCy, which provides a complete processing pipeline from tokenization to Named Entity Recognition.

On small corpora, spaCy performs very well and produces few clearly inconsistent errors. However, on longer texts, especially Asimov corpora, anomalies appear. To limit these errors, we use an anti-dictionary: a list of terms explicitly excluded from character recognition. This dictionary is progressively enriched during development as new unexpected words are identified. Its use must remain targeted, because authors do not always follow standard naming conventions. Asimov provides a notable example with the character name "Dors," which can be interpreted out of context as a common verb ("dors" is the third person singular of "dormir" in French). This creates a significant risk of false positives if the anti-dictionary is not used.

\subsection{Alias Management}

In a narrative, the same character can be referred to in multiple ways. These forms are grouped under the term alias. Identifying these forms and linking them to the correct character is one of the main challenges of automatic character network extraction.

At this stage, spaCy alone did not provide a satisfactory alias-handling solution. To address this limitation, we developed dedicated algorithms. In our pipeline, we build a dictionary named aliases\_map, where each key corresponds to an identified character and each value corresponds to one alias.

In the current implementation, only obvious aliases are considered: first name, last name, or a combination of both. This intentionally simplified approach has clear limitations. It does not yet handle cases such as characters sharing names, characters with multiple first or last names, and aliases not based on standard anthroponyms.

\subsection{Relationship Management}

The second major objective is to identify relationships between entities. This task is as complex as character identification itself. However, project guidelines simplify the approach by defining a relationship based on textual proximity.

Two characters are considered related when one occurrence of each is separated by 25 tokens or fewer. Although this heuristic can be debated, it is an acceptable compromise in this work.

To model relationships between identified characters, we build a dictionary named relations\_map. Each key corresponds to a pair of characters, and each value stores the number of detected relationship occurrences involving that pair.

\section{Future Steps}

This section presents improvements we plan to implement to strengthen our solution.

For obvious reasons, these improvements might change over time, depending on the results obtained and the difficulties encountered during implementation. They are therefore presented in a non-exhaustive manner, as a list of possible directions rather than a fixed roadmap.

\subsection{Alias Management}

Current alias handling relies on simple rules based on first and last names. To go beyond these limits, we plan a graph-based approach for modeling detected named entities.

Each named entity detected by the NER module will be represented as a node in a graph. For each pair of entities, we will compute two similarity dimensions.

The first is orthographic similarity, based on standard string distance or similarity metrics. This captures obvious cases, such as "Hari Seldon" and "Seldon," as well as small surface variations.

The second is contextual similarity, based on the linguistic contexts in which entities appear. Context windows around occurrences will be vectorized. Entities with similar contexts, for example co-occurring with similar verbs, places, or other characters, will have a higher probability of referring to the same individual.

After building the graph, we will identify clusters (strongly connected subsets). Each cluster will represent one character and aggregate corresponding aliases. This should provide better robustness for complex cases, especially when lexical variants are only indirectly connected.

Threshold values will be required both for edge creation and for deciding whether a cluster represents a single character. These thresholds will be set through empirical experiments.

From a technical perspective, graph construction can rely on NetworkX, clustering on Louvain-based community detection, textual similarity on RapidFuzz, and contextual similarity on SentenceTransformers.

\subsection{Relationship Management}

A relevant improvement is to introduce relation types (family, hierarchical, conflictual, collaborative, etc.) and automatically classify each detected relation.

We plan to define prototype sentences for each relation type. These manually built prototypes will represent characteristic interactions (for example: "X is the father of Y," "X ordered Y," "X fought Y"). For each category, prototype sentences will be vectorized and averaged to produce a central vector representation.

When a potential relation is detected with the 25-token rule, the corresponding sentence or sentences will be vectorized and compared to category mean vectors using cosine similarity. The relation will be assigned to the most similar category.

We may also define a neutral category for uncertain cases, which would require setting a similarity threshold.

This approach is relatively simple to implement while leveraging pre-trained semantic models. Its main limitation is possible bias due to a limited set of prototypes. Robustness will therefore need empirical evaluation, especially for indirect or implicit formulations common in literary texts.

Technically, SentenceTransformers will be used for vectorization, and public datasets may be used to collect prototype sentences.

\section{Conclusion}

This progress report presented the main stages of the pipeline developed for automatic extraction of character networks from Isaac Asimov's works. Initial results show that text preprocessing is essential for improving analysis quality, while existing NLP tools such as spaCy allow effective identification of part of the named entities.

However, important limitations remain, especially in alias handling and precise relationship identification, which currently rely on simple heuristics. A graph-based approach for alias disambiguation and semantic representations for relation classification should improve robustness.

Next steps are to implement these methods, adapt preprocessing to each corpus, and evaluate the impact of these improvements on global extraction quality.
